\begin{abstract}
Speculative decoding (SD) uses a small draft model to propose tokens that a
larger target model verifies in parallel. We study whether the location of
Korean subword boundaries is associated with draft--target rejection. Our
analysis replays greedy verification decisions from saved target continuations
and teacher-forced scores; a live-decoder validation agrees on 98.06\% of
eligible shared-context Q2 positions, above a preselected 95\% gate, while
unmatched and diverged contexts remain outside that claim. Across eight core
model configurations, adjusted CROSS--WITHIN rejection contrasts vary in sign
and size. A scalar boundary-F1 error does not show the hypothesized positive
association, and the six-pair Qwen grid does not support a uniform
nominal-to-particle effect. Token-level diagnostics locate many fused
nominal--particle boundaries within the token, but particle swaps are uncommon
in decoded draft candidates. RQ4 stress tests show sensitivity to label source,
frequency specification, and support; arbitrary pseudo-boundaries reproduce
most of the raw CROSS--WITHIN gap. These are observational replay results, not
causal effects or latency measurements. No human annotation was obtained; an
automatic MeCab-ko comparison is reported separately and is not treated as
human validation. At block size four, visible single-boundary CROSS positions
account for 35.08\% of replay rejections and 35.29\% of wasted draft work,
while the model-based counterfactual changes expected accepted length by only
0.025 target tokens per call.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Speculative decoding accelerates autoregressive generation by having a smaller
draft model propose a block that a larger target model verifies in parallel
\citep{leviathan2023,chen2023speculative}. Its practical value depends on the
draft's agreement with the target, and prior work has examined confidence-based
draft lengths and language-specific drafters \citep{zhang2025draft,yi2024multilingual}.
We ask whether the local linguistic structure of a token provides a useful
description of this agreement in Korean.

Korean eojeol commonly combine a lexical stem with particles or endings.
Frequency-trained subword tokenizers can split inside a morpheme or merge
characters that fall on both sides of a morpheme boundary. Research on Korean
tokenization has studied morphology-aware segmentation as a way to improve
language modeling and downstream tasks \citep{park2020korean,lee2026morpheme}.
Whether these boundary relationships are associated with greedy SD rejection
is a separate empirical question. We use a Korean morphological analyzer to
describe token spans, without changing the models or proposing a new decoding
method \citep{lee2024kiwi}.

The initial hypothesis was that a single scalar measure of token--morpheme
boundary mismatch would identify risky proposals. The completed full-sample
analysis does not support that simple account: the adjusted association for
boundary-F1 error is negative, and the fusion component is not distinguishable
from zero. Across model configurations, the adjusted global CROSS--WITHIN
contrast also changes sign. These results make it necessary to report both the
direction of the mismatch and the support available at each boundary.

We organize the analysis around five questions:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Where?} Does scalar token--morpheme alignment predict
    rejection, and which mismatch directions and boundaries are associated
    with it?
    \item \textbf{How general?} How do the observed patterns vary with draft
    and target scale, model family, domain, and generated versus human-written
    text?
    \item \textbf{Why?} Do token geometry, draft confidence, or particle
    choices describe the observed differences?
    \item \textbf{Is it morphology?} Do token identities, arbitrary cuts,
    frequency, proposal-side labeling, or a second analyzer change the result?
    \item \textbf{What is the replay cost?} What share of rejections and
    invalidated draft work is assigned to visible CROSS positions, and how
    large is a specified accepted-length counterfactual?
\end{enumerate}

This is an analysis paper. We make no causal claim about morphology and no
claim of measured acceleration. Our contributions are a directional analysis
of token-boundary associations across model settings, a set of mechanism and
robustness diagnostics, and a conditional accounting of replay rejections and
wasted draft work. The full configurations, artifacts, and execution logs are
available in the accompanying repository.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Speculative decoding.}
Speculative decoding uses a draft model to propose continuations and a target
model to verify them while preserving the target decoding rule
\citep{leviathan2023,chen2023speculative}. Adaptive draft-length work uses
model confidence or entropy to decide when to stop drafting
\citep{zhang2025draft}. Our study is complementary: we characterize
token-level rejection associations by the relationship between subword spans
and an automatic Korean morphological analysis. We do not evaluate an adaptive
policy or compare wall-clock decoding systems.

\paragraph{Korean morphology and tokenization.}
Korean tokenization studies compare subword and morphology-aware segmentation
strategies \citep{park2020korean,lee2026morpheme}. We use Kiwi to obtain
surface morpheme spans \citep{lee2024kiwi}. The analyzer supplies automatic
labels and is not treated as gold annotation. Our question concerns how these
labels align with target tokens and saved draft proposals during greedy replay,
not whether a morphology-aware tokenizer improves model quality.

\section{Experimental Setting}
\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Models and text}

The core study uses eight draft--target configurations: six Qwen3 pairs
(Q1--Q6), Gemma-3 4B$\rightarrow$12B (G1), and Ministral-3 3B$\rightarrow$8B
(M1). Q2 (Qwen3 0.6B$\rightarrow$4B) uses 40,000 frozen FineWeb2 Korean
prompts; the other core generated-text pairs use the common 20,000-prompt
split. We additionally score human-written Korean continuations for the eight
core pairs and run the Q2 and G1 pairs on a frozen 2,000-document Korean
Wikipedia sample.

Four optional extensions were completed: a Qwen3-14B target scale extension
(O1), a 5,000-document Japanese control with Qwen and Gemma pairs (O2), a
1,012-item FLORES-200 English-to-Korean prompt set (O3), and a Gemma-3-1B
draft paired with the existing Gemma-3-12B target (O4). These are exploratory
extensions and use saved-continuation replay.

\subsection{Saved-continuation replay and live validation}

For each target, we generate a greedy continuation of at most 128 tokens. We
then score that continuation under the target and each draft with teacher
forcing. For a block size $k$, CPU replay accepts draft argmaxes while they
match the saved target token, marks the first mismatch as a rejection, and
invalidates the remaining proposals in that block. No proposal is counted at a
bonus position. This reproduces greedy verification decisions conditional on
the saved continuation and matching runtime decisions; it does not measure
parallel verification latency.

We validate the replay with live SD for 1,000 Q2 prompts and 500 G1 prompts.
The prespecified gate is rejection-label agreement among eligible positions
where live decoding and the saved target continuation share the same context.
Q2 agreement is 98.0577\% over 58,385 positions, exceeding the user's 95\%
threshold. G1 agreement is 99.8213\% over 45,879 positions. The stricter
zero-unmatched diagnostic fails: Q2 has 949 unmatched live positions and 947
unmatched simulation positions in shared contexts. Across all contexts Q2
agreement is 88.65\%; only 54.58\% of target tokens belong to the common
prefix, and 40.5\% of prompts have identical target sequences. We therefore
scope replay claims to shared-context positions. The gate does not validate
diverged contexts or establish live latency.

\subsection{Morphological labels and analysis}

Kiwi aligns target-token spans to morphemes inside each eojeol. We classify a
visible token as \textsc{Cross} when its span contains an internal morpheme
boundary, and as \textsc{Within} when it splits within a morpheme without
crossing one. Exact-boundary, multi-boundary, ambiguous, and incomplete
alignments are handled as described in each analysis metadata file. Unless
specified otherwise, the main CROSS--WITHIN analyses use target-side token
labels along the saved continuation. They are retrospective labels for
positions in the target path; they are not labels of the rejected draft
candidate.

The separate proposal-side RQ4 analysis projects each eligible draft argmax
onto its saved target-prefix context using the pinned Qwen06 tokenizer. These
labels describe the actual saved-prefix candidate when projection succeeds.
The target-side and proposal-side estimates are reported separately because
they answer different measurement questions.

Pair-specific logistic models use the plan's common controls: eojeol-token
count and block-slot fixed effects, a five-degree cubic spline of generated
position, log eojeol length, draft and target entropy, and draft and target
top-two margins. Standard errors are clustered by document. Reported AMEs are
percentage-point differences in simulated rejection probability. These
adjusted observational associations are not causal effects. Human-written-text
analyses measure draft--target argmax disagreement on the document's real next
tokens; they are not live SD rejection outcomes.

\section{RQ2: Generality Across Models, Domains, and Text Sources}
\label{sec:rq2}

The core Qwen grid extends across three draft sizes and three target sizes.
Its adjusted global and nominal-to-particle (N$\rightarrow$P) contrasts
vary across the six pairs; the Qwen N$\rightarrow$P estimates range from
$-1.95$ to $+3.00$ pp, and several intervals include zero. The eight
generated-text core pairs also span Gemma and Ministral. Raw N$\rightarrow$P
differences in the common-pool family comparison are positive for Qwen and
Ministral, but not for Gemma. Figure~\ref{fig:heatmap} summarizes supported

We compare the ordering of fine-boundary estimates across 18 core settings:
eight generated-text pairs, eight corresponding human-written-text
disagreement settings, and two Wikipedia settings. Across 153 setting pairs,
136 have at least three shared supported boundaries. For core generated
settings, mean rank correlation is 0.618, sign agreement is 0.796, and
top-five Jaccard is 0.306. Same-pair generated-versus-human comparisons have
mean rank correlation 0.367, sign agreement 0.792, and top-five Jaccard
0.186. Human-text comparisons use disagreement on the same human-written
prefix and must not be read as replay rejection. Seventeen comparisons are
not estimable; all involve Wikipedia Q2, which has only one supported fine
boundary. The stability analysis therefore shows partial ordering agreement,
not a stable universal boundary ranking.

\paragraph{Optional scale and domain checks.}
The completed optional studies add scale and domain evidence, but their
different prompt sources and smaller samples make them exploratory. With a
Qwen3-14B target, the three Qwen draft sizes have global estimates from
$-1.258$ to $+0.466$ pp; their N$\rightarrow$P estimates are positive only
for the 0.6B draft. On 1,012 FLORES English prompts translated into Korean,
global and N$\rightarrow$P effects vary across eight pairs, and fine-boundary
rank stability is estimable only for Ministral (seven shared supported
boundaries; $\rho=0.86$). The 5,000-document Japanese control yields
different topic-particle and subject-particle patterns across Qwen and Gemma;
the Gemma subject-particle fit is separation-limited. The Gemma1-to-Gemma12 extension estimates
$-0.365$ pp globally and $-0.226$ pp for N$\rightarrow$particle, both below
zero. Full optional estimates and caveats are in Appendix~\ref{app:optional}.

\section{RQ3: Token Geometry and Candidate Diagnostics}
\label{sec:rq3}

Among 168,861 visible, aligned, single-boundary CROSS target tokens, the
internal N$\rightarrow$P boundary lies in the 25--50\% position bin for
85.9\% and in the 50--75\% bin for 14.1\%. This locates where the
morpheme boundary falls within a fused target token; it does not establish a
grammatical mechanism.

Among target-consistent CROSS rejection events, the rejected Qwen 0.6B
argmax ranks 2--5 in the saved Qwen4 target top-20 for 27,994 of 33,653
positions, ranks 6--10 for 3,467, ranks 11--20 for 1,315, and below rank 20
for 877 (right-censored). Median draft entropy is 2.565 nats for
target-consistent aligned CROSS rejections and 3.861 for WITHIN rejections.
These diagnostics describe score distributions and are not evidence that
entropy or geometry causes rejection.

The planned particle-confusion hypothesis predicted topic/subject swaps to
dominate rejected N$\rightarrow$P candidates. Instead, among 5,842
target-consistent, single-boundary events, Kiwi finds no particle in the
decoded draft candidate surface for 83.1\%; topic/subject swaps total 372
events. This surface diagnostic does not support the predicted dominance.
Descriptive draft-size slopes are negative for global and N$\rightarrow$P
contrasts under the 4B and 8B targets, but use only two or three draft sizes
and have no inferential confidence intervals.

\section{RQ4: Falsification and Analyzer Robustness}
\label{sec:rq4}

The full Q2 target-side adjusted estimate is near zero
($+0.041$ pp; 95\% CI $[-0.111,+0.192]$). The common-20k estimate and the
strict-alignment estimate are also near zero. Excluding multi-boundary CROSS
tokens changes the estimate little. These checks do not support a robust
positive global target-side contrast.

The pseudo-boundary experiment samples the same number of random internal
character cuts as Kiwi boundaries within each eligible eojeol. The observed
raw rejection-rate gap is $+8.64$ pp; the 100-draw pseudo-gap median is
$+7.39$ pp (95\% draw interval $[+7.30,+7.50]$), and none of the draws
reaches the observed gap. Thus arbitrary cuts reproduce most of the raw
difference, while the observed gap exceeds this pseudo distribution. This
weakens a morphology-specific reading; it is not a randomized causal test.

The estimate is specification-sensitive to frequency controls. Using an
external Wikipedia frequency cache gives negative adjusted estimates
($-1.86$ to $-1.91$ pp); a disjoint FineWeb2 frequency sample also gives
negative estimates ($-1.111$ pp for a spline and $-1.205$ pp for deciles).
The top-token removal fits rise to $+3.42$--$+4.54$ pp after removing the
top 25--100 CROSS token IDs per boundary, but CROSS support falls from
149,457 rows to 1,384--307. These sparse stress tests do not establish a
population-level effect after token removal.

The proposal-side refit produces a different estimate: projecting saved
draft argmaxes on their target-prefix context gives a CROSS--WITHIN AME of
$+4.773$ pp (95\% CI $[+4.657,+4.889]$) on 1,914,148 valid proposal-side
rows from 39,311 documents. Proposal-side and target-side class labels
agree on 99.42\% of 1,528,036 comparable rows. This high class agreement
does not make the estimands interchangeable: the result remains conditional
on successful saved-prefix projection and the analysis filters.

MeCab-ko parsed 162,137 unique eojeol strings. Its automatic second-analyzer
CROSS--WITHIN AME is $-0.037$ pp (95\% CI $[-0.192,+0.118]$, $p=.639$)
on 1,860,584 strict-alignment proposals. This is not human validation. The
planned 300-item annotation sheet is prepared, but all annotator fields are
blank because no annotator was available by the plan's deadline. We use the
planned MeCab fallback as an automatic comparison and retain the missing
human review as a limitation.

\section{RQ5: Rejection and Draft-Work Accounting}
\label{sec:rq5}

At block size four, the Q2 replay contains 717,681 rejection events and
2,128,451 units of wasted draft work under the planned accounting, which
charges the rejected proposal and every invalidated suffix proposal to the
first rejected position. Five coarse single-boundary CROSS families account
for 251,794 rejections (35.08\%) and 751,148 wasted draft tokens (35.29\%).
N$\rightarrow$P alone accounts for 90,193 rejections (12.57\%) and 273,076
wasted draft tokens (12.83\%). The \texttt{OTHER\_OR\_UNALIGNED} bucket
accounts for 64.77\% of rejections, limiting attribution to the visible
aligned subset.

The counterfactual replaces each CROSS position's observed mismatch with an
independent Bernoulli outcome whose probability equals the eligible
WITHIN rate for that coarse boundary; other positions retain their observed
mismatch labels. At $k=4$, observed replay yields 3.452 target tokens per
target call; the counterfactual is 3.477 (document-cluster 95\% CI
$[3.471,3.483]$), a difference of $+0.025$ tokens per call. Differences
for $k=1,2,4,8,16$ are $+0.003,+0.009,+0.025,+0.054,+0.084$,
respectively. This is a model-based counterfactual, not measured latency or
a causal estimate. Its intervals hold estimated WITHIN rates fixed and do
not include uncertainty in those rates; positions are treated as independent
conditional on boundary family.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Across the completed experiments, the evidence does not support a universal
claim that crossing a Korean morpheme boundary raises greedy SD rejection.
The full Q2 scalar-alignment model is not positive, core global contrasts
vary across model settings, the pseudo-boundary experiment reproduces most
of the raw gap, and frequency adjustments are specification-sensitive.
Proposal-side labeling yields a positive adjusted contrast, but uses a
different label source and selected projection population. These results
support describing local tokenization structure as a measurement axis for
draft--target disagreement, while keeping the claim conditional on the
analysis and replay scope.

The particle diagnostics do not establish a grammatical-choice mechanism:
most decoded candidate surfaces receive no particle label, and topic/subject
swaps are not dominant. The RQ5 accounting assigns roughly one third of
rejections and wasted draft work to visible single-boundary CROSS families,
but the majority of rejections are in the other-or-unaligned bucket. The
accepted-length counterfactual is modest and model-based. Accordingly, the
results motivate future tests of tokenization-aware risk prediction and
drafting policies, but do not demonstrate that such a policy improves
throughput.

\section{Implications and Future Directions}
\label{sec:implications}

Future work can test whether a prefix-available boundary signal adds
predictive value beyond draft confidence on held-out prompts, whether
boundary-aware draft length changes accepted work under live SD, and whether
the observations transfer to other agglutinative languages. The Japanese
control is exploratory and cannot establish cross-language generality.
Tokenizer designs that preserve frequent grammatical units, distillation on
particle decisions, and adaptive drafting are hypotheses for future
experiments, not findings of this study.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

First, the design is observational. Regression adjustment does not identify
a causal effect of morphology, token identity, or tokenization. Second, the
main labels come from Kiwi and remain automatically generated. The planned
300-item human validation was not completed because no annotator was
available. MeCab-ko provides an automatic second-analyzer comparison, not a
substitute for expert judgment.

Third, the replay is conditional on saved target continuations. Q2 shared-
context rejection-label agreement passes the 95\% gate, but the strict
zero-unmatched check fails; the 54.58\% common-prefix fraction and 88.65\%
all-context agreement show why claims are limited to shared contexts. Fourth,
no live latency or throughput is measured, and the cost counterfactual relies
on fixed estimated WITHIN rates and conditional independence. Fifth, results
use base models and a finite collection of Korean prompts, human-written text,
Wikipedia, and translated FLORES prompts. Optional Japanese and FLORES
extensions are small and exploratory. Finally, the particle confusion
analysis uses automatic surface labels and does not establish a causal
linguistic mechanism.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We completed a five-question analysis of Korean token--morpheme alignment and
greedy speculative-decoding replay. The principal result is heterogeneity:
scalar boundary error is not positively associated with rejection, global
CROSS--WITHIN contrasts change across settings, and robustness estimates
depend on label source and specification. The replay cost analysis assigns
35.08\% of observed rejections to visible single-boundary CROSS families at
$k=4$, while its counterfactual accepted-length difference is only
$+0.025$ target tokens per call. These results describe saved-continuation
associations and motivate further live, held-out evaluation; they do not
show a causal morphology effect or a measured speedup.

\section{Reproducibility and artifacts}
\label{app:repro}

All planned core and optional model jobs completed: V0, the Qwen grid, Gemma
and Ministral target units, teacher-forced human-text scoring, the
plan-approved 2,000-document Wikipedia sample, and optional O1--O4. No model

annotation was not completed because no annotator was available; the 300-row

outputs, model revisions, command logs, and per-job timing are recorded in the
repository. The RQ1--RQ5 result tables and figures can be reproduced with the

contains 300 stratified rows with blank annotator fields. All model-run and
log artifacts are available at
